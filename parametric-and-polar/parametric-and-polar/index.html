Part 9 · Parametric Equations, Polar Coordinates, and Vector-Valued Functions

Parametric, polar, and vector-valued functions

Three ways to describe a curve that no y = f(x) can, and the calculus that goes with each.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Find dy/dx for a parametric curve
  • Compute arc length and speed from a parametrisation
  • Convert between polar and Cartesian, and find polar area
  • Treat a vector-valued function as a position over time

A function y=f(x)y = f(x)y=f(x) cannot describe a circle, a spiral, or any path that doubles back — one xxx gets one yyy, and those curves refuse. Three representations get around it, and all three carry the same idea: describe the curve by something other than xxx.

Parametric curves

Give both coordinates as functions of a parameter, usually ttt:

x=f(t),y=g(t)x = f(t), \qquad y = g(t) x=f(t),y=g(t)

A circle becomes effortless: x=cos⁡tx = \cos tx=cost, y=sin⁡ty = \sin ty=sint.

The first line confirms the path really is the unit circle; the other two are the velocity components.

The derivative comes from the chain rule, with dtdtdt cancelling formally:

dydx=dy/dtdx/dt\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt} dxdy​=dx/dtdy/dt​

For the circle that is cos⁡t−sin⁡t=−cot⁡t\tfrac{\cos t}{-\sin t} = -\cot t−sintcost​=−cott — undefined at t=0t = 0t=0, where the curve is vertical, which is correct and is the kind of thing a Cartesian description handles badly.

The second derivative is the one students get wrong:

d2ydx2=ddt(dydx)dxdt\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)}{\frac{dx}{dt}} dx2d2y​=dtdx​dtd​(dxdy​)​

Pitfall

The second derivative is not d2y/dt2d2x/dt2\tfrac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}d2x/dt2d2y/dt2​.

Differentiate dydx\tfrac{dy}{dx}dxdy​ with respect to ttt, then divide by dxdt\tfrac{dx}{dt}dtdx​ again — because you are differentiating with respect to xxx, and each such differentiation costs another division by dxdt\tfrac{dx}{dt}dtdx​.

Writing the ratio of second derivatives is the standard wrong answer and it is never right.

Speed and arc length

A parametrisation is naturally a motion, and its velocity components are dxdt\tfrac{dx}{dt}dtdx​ and dydt\tfrac{dy}{dt}dtdy​. Speed is their magnitude:

speed=(dxdt)2+(dydt)2\text{speed} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2} speed=(dtdx​)2+(dtdy​)2​

and arc length is speed integrated over time — distance is speed times time, accumulated:

L=∫ab(dxdt)2+(dydt)2 dtL = \int_a^b \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt L=∫ab​(dtdx​)2+(dtdy​)2​dt

For the unit circle the speed is sin⁡2t+cos⁡2t=1\sqrt{\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t} = 1sin2t+cos2t​=1 — constant — so the arc length from 000 to 2π2\pi2π is 2π2\pi2π. The circumference falls out of the integral, which is a reassuring check on the formula.

Note

Speed constant does not mean velocity constant. On the circle the speed is 111 throughout while the direction changes continuously — exactly the situation in PhysTB’s circular-motion chapters, where a force changes direction without changing speed.

Polar coordinates

Locate a point by distance and angle rather than by two distances:

x=rcos⁡θ,y=rsin⁡θx = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta x=rcosθ,y=rsinθ

r2=x2+y2,tan⁡θ=yxr^2 = x^2 + y^2, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x} r2=x2+y2,tanθ=xy​

Vector-valued functions

Bundle the parametrisation into one object:

r⃗(t)=⟨x(t), y(t)⟩\vec r(t) = \langle x(t),\, y(t)\rangle r(t)=⟨x(t),y(t)⟩
