Part 7 · Differential Equations

Differential equations

An equation whose unknown is a function. Separation turns it back into two ordinary integrals.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Verify that a function solves a differential equation
  • Solve a separable equation and apply an initial condition
  • Model exponential and logistic growth
  • Read a solution curve off a slope field

Every equation so far had a number for its unknown. A differential equation has a function for its unknown, and describes it by how it changes:

dydx=2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 2y dxdy​=2y

“Find the function whose rate of change is twice its value.” That is a description of behaviour, not of a value, which is why differential equations are how science states its laws — chapters 2.1 and 10.1 of PhysTB are both exactly this.

Checking a solution is easy

Solving is work; verifying is just differentiating.

Is y=3e2xy = 3e^{2x}y=3e2x a solution of dydx=2y\tfrac{dy}{dx} = 2ydxdy​=2y?

Both sides come to 6e2x6e^{2x}6e2x, so yes. Note that 5e2x5e^{2x}5e2x works too, and so does Ce2xCe^{2x}Ce2x for any constant — a differential equation has a family of solutions, and pinning down which one needs an extra fact.

That extra fact is an initial condition like y(0)=3y(0) = 3y(0)=3, and a problem giving you one is asking for a single function rather than a family.

Separation of variables

The one technique AB needs. When the equation can be written with all the $y$s on one side and all the $x$s on the other, integrate both sides.

For dydx=2y\tfrac{dy}{dx} = 2ydxdy​=2y:

dyy=2 dx⟹∫dyy=∫2 dx⟹ln⁡∣y∣=2x+C\frac{dy}{y} = 2\,dx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int\frac{dy}{y} = \int 2\,dx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln|y| = 2x + Cydy​=2dx⟹∫ydy​=∫2dx⟹ln∣y∣=2x+C

Exponentiating gives y=Ce2xy = Ce^{2x}y=Ce2x, and y(0)=3y(0) = 3y(0)=3 makes C=3C = 3C=3.

Where the constant goes. One constant is enough — combining the two from the separate integrals gives a single CCC, and exponentiating turns an additive constant into a multiplicative one. That is why the answer has CCC as a coefficient rather than an addend.

Pitfall

Apply the initial condition after integrating and before simplifying further, and remember that eCe^{C}eC is a new constant rather than something to carry around.

The commonest error is forgetting CCC entirely, which produces one particular solution and quietly loses the family. On an FRQ that is most of the marks.

Exponential growth

dydt=ky\tfrac{dy}{dt} = kydtdy​=ky says the rate of growth is proportional to the amount present, and its solution is

y=y0ekty = y_0 e^{kt} y=y0​ekt

Positive kkk is growth, negative is decay — the second claim is the decay case heading to zero. This one equation covers compound interest, population growth without constraint, and radioactive decay.

Doubling and half-life. Time to double satisfies ekt=2e^{kt} = 2ekt=2, so t=ln⁡2kt = \tfrac{\ln 2}{k}t=kln2​ — independent of where you started, which is the characteristic property of exponential behaviour and a standard exam question.

Logistic growth

Unconstrained exponential growth is unphysical: populations run out of food. The logistic equation adds a ceiling LLL:

dydt=ky(1−yL)\frac{dy}{dt} = ky\left(1 - \frac{y}{L}\right) dtdy​=ky(1−Ly​)

The bracket is near 111 when yyy is small — so growth starts exponential — and near 000 as yyy approaches LLL, which stalls it. The population levels off at LLL, the carrying capacity.

Two facts the exam asks for directly, and both come from the equation without solving it:

  • lim⁡t→∞y=L\lim_{t\to\infty} y = Llimt→∞​y=L. Growth stops when the bracket vanishes.
  • Growth is fastest at y=L/2y = L/2y=L/2. The rate ky(1−y/L)ky(1 - y/L)ky(1−y/L) is a downward parabola in yyy, and its vertex is halfway.

Setting that derivative to zero gives y=L2y = \tfrac{L}{2}y=2L​ exactly — the maximum growth rate, verified. That point is the inflection point of the solution curve, where it stops curving up and starts curving down.

Slope fields

A slope field draws a short segment at each point with the slope the equation prescribes there. Solution curves follow the segments, and one passes through each point — which is a picture of the family of solutions all at once.

Reading one:

  • Follow the segments from the initial condition to sketch that solution.
  • Horizontal segments mark where dydx=0\tfrac{dy}{dx} = 0dxdy​=0 — equilibria.
  • Segments depending only on yyy mean the field looks the same at every xxx, which is the signature of an autonomous equation like dydt=ky\tfrac{dy}{dt} = kydtdy​=ky.

For the logistic equation the field is horizontal along y=0y = 0y=0 and y=Ly = Ly=L — the two equilibria — steepest in between, and every curve starting between them climbs toward LLL.

Recap

  • A differential equation’s unknown is a function; verifying a proposed solution is just differentiation.
  • Solutions come in families. An initial condition picks one out — losing CCC loses the family.
  • Separate the variables, integrate both sides, then apply the condition.
  • dydt=ky\tfrac{dy}{dt} = kydtdy​=ky gives y=y0ekty = y_0e^{kt}y=y0​ekt; doubling time is ln⁡2k\tfrac{\ln 2}{k}kln2​, independent of the starting amount.
  • Logistic growth levels off at LLL and is fastest at L2\tfrac{L}{2}2L​, which is the solution curve’s inflection point.
  • A slope field shows the whole family at once; follow the segments from the initial condition.
