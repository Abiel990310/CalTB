Part 3 · Differentiation: Composite, Implicit, and Inverse Functions

Implicit differentiation

Differentiating an equation you cannot solve for y — which is most equations, and the technique behind related rates and inverse functions.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Differentiate an equation implicitly and solve for dy/dx
  • Explain why every y produces a dy/dx factor
  • Find the tangent line to an implicitly defined curve
  • Derive the derivative of an inverse function

x2+y2=25x^2 + y^2 = 25x2+y2=25 is a circle, and no function describes it — a circle fails the vertical line test. Yet it plainly has a tangent line at every point, so a derivative exists locally even though yyy is not a function of xxx globally.

Implicit differentiation gets that derivative without ever solving for yyy.

The one rule

Differentiate both sides with respect to xxx, treating yyy as a function of xxx. Every yyy you differentiate produces a factor of dydx\tfrac{dy}{dx}dxdy​, by the chain rule.

For x2+y2=25x^2 + y^2 = 25x2+y2=25:

2x+2ydydx=0⟹dydx=−xy2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y} 2x+2ydxdy​=0⟹dxdy​=−yx​

The second line is where the dydx\tfrac{dy}{dx}dxdy​ comes from: differentiating y2y^2y2 with respect to yyy gives 2y2y2y, and the chain rule multiplies by dydx\tfrac{dy}{dx}dxdy​ to convert it to a derivative with respect to xxx.

Pitfall

The dydx\tfrac{dy}{dx}dxdy​ factor is the entire technique, and omitting it is the mistake that defines this topic. Writing 2x+2y=02x + 2y = 02x+2y=0 treats yyy as a constant, which it is not — yyy changes when xxx does, and saying so is the chain rule.

Test yourself: ddx(y3)\tfrac{d}{dx}(y^3)dxd​(y3) is 3y2dydx3y^2\tfrac{dy}{dx}3y2dxdy​, not 3y23y^23y2.

Checking against the explicit route

The circle is one of the rare cases where you can solve for yyy, which makes it a good check. The upper half is y=25−x2y = \sqrt{25 - x^2}y=25−x2​:

That is −xy-\tfrac{x}{y}−yx​, since y=25−x2y = \sqrt{25-x^2}y=25−x2​ on the upper half. The two routes agree, and the implicit one gave the answer in one line without a square root and without splitting the circle into halves.

That economy is the point. For x3+y3=6xyx^3 + y^3 = 6xyx3+y3=6xy — the folium of Descartes — solving for yyy means a cubic formula. Differentiating implicitly takes a line:

3x2+3y2dydx=6y+6xdydx3x^2 + 3y^2\frac{dy}{dx} = 6y + 6x\frac{dy}{dx} 3x2+3y2dxdy​=6y+6xdxdy​

Collect the dydx\tfrac{dy}{dx}dxdy​ terms on one side and factor:

dydx=6y−3x23y2−6x\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{6y - 3x^2}{3y^2 - 6x} dxdy​=3y2−6x6y−3x2​

The answer depends on both xxx and yyy, which is normal for an implicit derivative and is exactly right — a curve that doubles back has different slopes at two points with the same xxx.

Products need the product rule

A term with both variables needs the product rule and the chain rule:

ddx(xy)=1⋅y+xdydx\frac{d}{dx}(xy) = 1\cdot y + x\frac{dy}{dx} dxd​(xy)=1⋅y+xdxdy​

That verified line is the first half — the derivative of xyxyxy treating yyy as constant is yyy — and the second half, xdydxx\tfrac{dy}{dx}xdxdy​, is what implicit differentiation adds. Missing it is the same omission as before, wearing a different hat.

Tangent lines

The usual exam question: find the tangent to x2+y2=25x^2 + y^2 = 25x2+y2=25 at (3,4)(3, 4)(3,4).

dydx=−xy=−34\tfrac{dy}{dx} = -\tfrac{x}{y} = -\tfrac{3}{4}dxdy​=−yx​=−43​, so the tangent is

y−4=−34(x−3)y - 4 = -\tfrac34(x - 3) y−4=−43​(x−3)

Sanity check: the radius to (3,4)(3,4)(3,4) has slope 43\tfrac4334​, and −34-\tfrac34−43​ is its negative reciprocal — the tangent is perpendicular to the radius, as a circle’s tangent must be. Geometry confirming calculus is a free check, and worth taking.

Inverse functions

Implicit differentiation gives the inverse-function rule immediately. If y=f−1(x)y = f^{-1}(x)y=f−1(x) then f(y)=xf(y) = xf(y)=x, and differentiating both sides:

f′(y)dydx=1⟹dydx=1f′(y)f'(y)\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{f'(y)} f′(y)dxdy​=1⟹dxdy​=f′(y)1​

The derivative of an inverse is the reciprocal of the original’s derivative, evaluated at the corresponding point.

That is where the inverse trigonometric derivatives come from:

Then cos⁡y=1−sin⁡2y=1−x2\cos y = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 y} = \sqrt{1 - x^2}cosy=1−sin2y​=1−x2​, and the standard result drops out.

The third line is the same trick on ey=xe^y = xey=x, which is why ln⁡\lnln differentiates to 1x\tfrac1xx1​ — a result that looks like it should need its own proof and does not.

Recap

  • Differentiate both sides with respect to xxx, treating yyy as a function of xxx. Every yyy differentiated owes a dydx\tfrac{dy}{dx}dxdy​.
  • Collect the dydx\tfrac{dy}{dx}dxdy​ terms, factor, and divide.
  • The answer usually contains both xxx and yyy, which is correct — a curve can have two slopes at one xxx.
  • A term like xyxyxy needs the product rule as well as the chain rule.
  • The inverse-function rule ddxf−1=1f′(y)\tfrac{d}{dx}f^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{f'(y)}dxd​f−1=f′(y)1​ falls out of differentiating f(y)=xf(y) = xf(y)=x, and with it every inverse trig derivative.
