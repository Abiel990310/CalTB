Part 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change

Substitution

The chain rule run backwards. Recognising when it applies is most of the skill; executing it is bookkeeping.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Recognise an integrand that is a chain rule derivative
  • Carry out a u-substitution, including changing the limits
  • Choose u when several candidates present themselves
  • Say why a substitution sometimes fails

Every differentiation rule runs backwards into an integration technique. The chain rule runs backwards into substitution, which is the one technique AB needs and the foundation of every technique BC adds.

The chain rule says

ddxF(u(x))=F′(u(x))⋅u′(x)\frac{d}{dx}F\big(u(x)\big) = F'\big(u(x)\big)\cdot u'(x) dxd​F(u(x))=F′(u(x))⋅u′(x)

Read right to left: anything of the form f(u)⋅u′f(u)\cdot u'f(u)⋅u′ integrates to F(u)F(u)F(u). The whole method is recognising that shape.

The shape to look for

An integrand that contains a function and its own derivative, up to a constant factor.

∫2xcos⁡(x2) dx\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx ∫2xcos(x2)dx

The inside is x2x^2x2; its derivative is 2x2x2x; and 2x2x2x is sitting right there. Substitute u=x2u = x^2u=x2, so du=2x dxdu = 2x\,dxdu=2xdx, and the integral becomes ∫cos⁡u du=sin⁡u=sin⁡(x2)\int\cos u\,du = \sin u = \sin(x^2)∫cosudu=sinu=sin(x2).

Both directions checked. The second line is the honest test of any integration: differentiate your answer and see whether you get the integrand back. That takes seconds and catches nearly every mistake, which makes skipping it inexcusable on an exam.

Working the bookkeeping

∫x ex2 dx\int x\,e^{x^2}\,dx ∫xex2dx

The derivative of x2x^2x2 is 2x2x2x and only xxx is present — off by a constant, which is always fixable.

Let u=x2u = x^2u=x2, so du=2x dxdu = 2x\,dxdu=2xdx, hence x dx=12 dux\,dx = \tfrac12\,duxdx=21​du:

∫xex2dx=12∫eu du=12eu=12ex2\int x e^{x^2}dx = \tfrac12\int e^u\,du = \tfrac12 e^u = \tfrac12 e^{x^2} ∫xex2dx=21​∫eudu=21​eu=21​ex2

Pitfall

A constant mismatch is fixable; a variable one is not.

∫xex2dx\int x e^{x^2}dx∫xex2dx works because the missing factor is the number 222, which moves freely across the integral sign.

∫ex2dx\int e^{x^2}dx∫ex2dx does not work, and no substitution rescues it — this integral has no elementary antiderivative at all. Not “hard”; impossible in elementary functions.

You cannot divide by a variable to fix a mismatch. ∫x2ex2dx\int x^2 e^{x^2}dx∫x2ex2dx is not x2ex2\tfrac{x}{2}e^{x^2}2x​ex2, because xxx cannot be moved through the integral sign.

Definite integrals: change the limits

With definite integrals there are two routes, and one is reliably safer.

Substitute the limits too. When u=x2u = x^2u=x2 and xxx runs from 000 to 222, then uuu runs from 000 to 444:

∫02xex2dx=12∫04eu du=12(e4−1)\int_0^2 x e^{x^2}dx = \tfrac12\int_0^4 e^u\,du = \tfrac12\left(e^4 - 1\right) ∫02​xex2dx=21​∫04​eudu=21​(e4−1)

Or convert back to xxx first, then use the original limits. Both are correct; the first is less error-prone, because forgetting to convert back is a standard way to evaluate the antiderivative at the wrong numbers.

Undefined behaviour

If you change the limits, do not substitute back to xxx as well. Doing both evaluates 12ex2\tfrac12 e^{x^2}21​ex2 at 000 and 444 instead of 000 and 222, and produces an answer that is wrong by an enormous margin while looking like ordinary work.

Pick one route per problem and finish it.

Choosing uuu

When more than one candidate presents itself, these heuristics hold up:

  • The inside of a composition. In cos⁡(x2)\cos(x^2)cos(x2), take u=x2u = x^2u=x2.
  • Whatever is under a root, inside a denominator, or in an exponent.
  • The thing whose derivative is also present, which is the real criterion — the others are shortcuts to it.

A quick test before committing: compute dududu and see whether the rest of the integrand is exactly dududu up to a constant. If a stray xxx survives, that choice of uuu is wrong. Trying the other candidate takes fifteen seconds.

Those three are all the same move. In the first, u=ln⁡xu = \ln xu=lnx gives du=dxxdu = \tfrac{dx}{x}du=xdx​, which is exactly what remains. In the second, tan⁡x=sin⁡xcos⁡x\tan x = \tfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}tanx=cosxsinx​ and u=cos⁡xu = \cos xu=cosx gives du=−sin⁡x dxdu = -\sin x\,dxdu=−sinxdx. In the third, u=x2+1u = x^2 + 1u=x2+1 gives du=2x dxdu = 2x\,dxdu=2xdx, off by the usual constant.

Once the pattern is visible, these stop being separate problems.
