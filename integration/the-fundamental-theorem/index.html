Part 6 · Integration and Accumulation of Change

The Fundamental Theorem

Differentiation and integration are inverse operations. That is the theorem, and it is why nobody computes a Riemann sum by hand twice.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • State both parts of the Fundamental Theorem
  • Evaluate a definite integral using an antiderivative
  • Differentiate a function defined by an integral, including with the chain rule
  • Explain why the theorem is true, not just what it says

Chapter 6.1 defined the integral as a limit of sums, which is honest and unusable — nobody evaluates ∫02x2 dx\int_0^2 x^2\,dx∫02​x2dx by summing rectangles. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is the shortcut, and calling it fundamental is not decoration. It says that the two central operations of the subject undo each other.

Part 2: evaluating integrals

Most-used first. If FFF is any antiderivative of fff — that is, F′=fF' = fF′=f — then

∫abf(x) dx=F(b)−F(a)\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) ∫ab​f(x)dx=F(b)−F(a)

So ∫02x2 dx=233−033=83\int_0^2 x^2\,dx = \tfrac{2^3}{3} - \tfrac{0^3}{3} = \tfrac83∫02​x2dx=323​−303​=38​ — the number the rectangles were converging on, obtained in one line.

Any antiderivative works. x33\tfrac{x^3}{3}3x3​ and x33+17\tfrac{x^3}{3} + 173x3​+17 are both antiderivatives of x2x^2x2, and the constant cancels in the subtraction: (F(b)+C)−(F(a)+C)=F(b)−F(a)(F(b) + C) - (F(a) + C) = F(b) - F(a)(F(b)+C)−(F(a)+C)=F(b)−F(a). That is why the +C+C+C that matters so much for indefinite integrals is irrelevant for definite ones.

Part 1: the derivative of an accumulation

Define a function by an integral with a variable upper limit:

g(x)=∫axf(t) dtg(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt g(x)=∫ax​f(t)dt

Then g′(x)=f(x)g'(x) = f(x)g′(x)=f(x).

Read that slowly, because it is the surprising half. Accumulating fff and then differentiating gives fff back. Integration and differentiation are inverses, and this is the precise statement of it.

Note

The variable of integration must differ from the limit. Writing ∫axf(x) dx\int_a^x f(x)\,dx∫ax​f(x)dx uses xxx for two different things — a bound and a dummy variable being summed over — and while a marker will usually let it pass, it stops making sense the moment you differentiate. Use ttt inside.

Why it is true, in one sentence: g(x+h)−g(x)g(x + h) - g(x)g(x+h)−g(x) is the area of a thin strip of width hhh and height about f(x)f(x)f(x), so the difference quotient g(x+h)−g(x)h\tfrac{g(x+h) - g(x)}{h}hg(x+h)−g(x)​ is about f(x)f(x)f(x), and exactly f(x)f(x)f(x) in the limit.

That is the whole proof, and it explains the theorem rather than asserting it: the rate at which accumulated area grows is the height of the curve at the edge where it is growing.

The properties you get for free

All of these follow from the definition and are worth recognising rather than deriving under time pressure:

∫aaf=0∫baf=−∫abf∫acf=∫abf+∫bcf\int_a^a f = 0 \qquad \int_b^a f = -\int_a^b f \qquad \int_a^c f = \int_a^b f + \int_b^c f∫aa​f=0∫ba​f=−∫ab​f∫ac​f=∫ab​f+∫bc​f

The middle one is a convention, and it is the right convention: it makes the third hold even when bbb lies outside [a,c][a, c][a,c], which keeps the algebra uniform instead of full of special cases.

Linearity comes across from differentiation unchanged — integrals split over sums and let constants out front — because the sums defining them do.

Pitfall

The theorem needs fff continuous on [a,b][a,b][a,b].

∫−111x2 dx\int_{-1}^{1}\tfrac{1}{x^2}\,dx∫−11​x21​dx looks like it evaluates to [−1x]−11=−1−1=−2\left[-\tfrac1x\right]_{-1}^{1} = -1 - 1 = -2[−x1​]−11​=−1−1=−2, which is nonsense: the integrand is positive everywhere it is defined, so no correct answer is negative.

The function has an infinite discontinuity at 000, inside the interval, so the theorem simply does not apply. Mechanically applying it to a discontinuous integrand produces confident nonsense, and the negative sign is the only clue that anything went wrong.

Recap

  • Part 2: ∫abf=F(b)−F(a)\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)∫ab​f=F(b)−F(a) for any antiderivative FFF. The constant cancels, which is why +C+C+C does not matter for definite integrals.
  • Part 1: ddx∫axf(t) dt=f(x)\tfrac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)dxd​∫ax​f(t)dt=f(x) — accumulating then differentiating returns the integrand.
  • The reason: the thin strip added between xxx and x+hx+hx+h has height about f(x)f(x)f(x), so the rate of accumulation is the height of the curve.
  • A variable upper limit brings the chain rule: multiply by u′(x)u'(x)u′(x). A variable lower limit does the same with a minus sign.
  • The theorem requires continuity on the interval. Applied across an infinite discontinuity it returns confident nonsense.
