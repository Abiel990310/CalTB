Part 5 · Analytical Applications of Differentiation

Extrema and the shape of a graph

The first derivative finds the turning points, the second decides which kind they are, and together they draw the curve.

By the end of this chapter you can

  • Find critical points and classify them
  • Use the first and second derivative tests, and know when each fails
  • Locate inflection points and relate them to concavity
  • Apply the Extreme Value Theorem on a closed interval

A derivative is a slope, so it knows where a curve is rising, falling, and momentarily doing neither. That last case is where maxima and minima live, and extracting the full shape of a graph from f′f'f′ and f′′f''f′′ is one of the most heavily tested skills in AB.

Critical points

ccc is a critical point when f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0f′(c)=0 or f′(c)f'(c)f′(c) does not exist.

Those are the only places an interior extremum can occur — which is worth stating precisely, because the converse is false and the exam tests the difference.

For x3−3xx^3 - 3xx3−3x, setting 3x2−3=03x^2 - 3 = 03x2−3=0 gives x=±1x = \pm 1x=±1: two critical points, and both turn out to be extrema.

For x3x^3x3, setting 3x2=03x^2 = 03x2=0 gives x=0x = 0x=0 — a critical point that is not an extremum. The curve flattens momentarily and carries straight on up.

Pitfall

f′(c)=0f'(c) = 0f′(c)=0 does not imply an extremum at ccc. x3x^3x3 at the origin is the standard counterexample: zero slope, no maximum, no minimum.

Critical points are candidates. Something else has to decide.

The first derivative test

Look at the sign of f′f'f′ on either side of the critical point:

f′f'f′ goes at ccc there is
+++ then −-− a local maximum
−-− then +++ a local minimum
no sign change neither

This is the test that always works, and it is the one to fall back on. It explains x3x^3x3 immediately: f′=3x2f' = 3x^2f′=3x2 is positive on both sides of zero, no sign change, so no extremum.

The second derivative test

Faster when it applies:

f′(c)=0 and f′′(c)>0  ⇒  minimumf'(c) = 0 \text{ and } f''(c) > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{minimum} f′(c)=0 and f′′(c)>0⇒minimum

f′(c)=0 and f′′(c)<0  ⇒  maximumf'(c) = 0 \text{ and } f''(c) < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{maximum} f′(c)=0 and f′′(c)<0⇒maximum

For x3−3xx^3 - 3xx3−3x, f′′=6xf'' = 6xf′′=6x. At x=1x = 1x=1 it is +6+6+6: a minimum. At x=−1x = -1x=−1 it is −6-6−6: a maximum. Two substitutions and the classification is done.

Undefined behaviour

When f′′(c)=0f''(c) = 0f′′(c)=0 the test says nothing at all. Not “neither” — nothing.

x4x^4x4 has f′′(0)=0f''(0) = 0f′′(0)=0 and a clear minimum. −x4-x^4−x4 has f′′(0)=0f''(0) = 0f′′(0)=0 and a clear maximum. x3x^3x3 has f′′(0)=0f''(0) = 0f′′(0)=0 and neither. All three look identical to the second derivative test, which is why an inconclusive result means going back to the first derivative test rather than guessing.

At x=0x = 0x=0 that second derivative is 000 — and x4x^4x4 plainly has a minimum there. Verified, and a reminder that the fast test is not the reliable one.

Concavity and inflection

f′′f''f′′ describes how the slope itself is changing:

  • f′′>0f'' > 0f′′>0 — slope increasing — concave up, curving like a cup.
  • f′′<0f'' < 0f′′<0 — slope decreasing — concave down, like a cap.

An inflection point is where concavity changes sign. As with extrema, f′′=0f'' = 0f′′=0 is necessary but not sufficient: x4x^4x4 has f′′(0)=0f''(0) = 0f′′(0)=0 and no inflection there, because f′′=12x2f'' = 12x^2f′′=12x2 never changes sign.

The pattern repeats one level up, and noticing that is worth more than memorising both cases: a zero of a derivative is a candidate, and a sign change is the evidence.

Drag hhh towards zero with the pivot at x=−1x = -1x=−1: the secant slope heads for zero, which is what a turning point looks like from the derivative’s side.

The Extreme Value Theorem

If fff is continuous on a closed interval [a,b][a,b][a,b], it attains an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum somewhere on it.

That guarantee makes the closed-interval method work:

  1. Find the critical points inside (a,b)(a, b)(a,b).
  2. Evaluate fff at those, and at both endpoints.
  3. The largest value is the absolute maximum; the smallest is the minimum.

No derivative test is needed — comparing the numbers settles it.

Pitfall

Forgetting the endpoints is the most common error in this procedure. On a closed interval the extreme value often occurs at an endpoint, where the derivative is not zero and no critical-point search will find it.

And the theorem needs closed. On the open interval (0,1)(0,1)(0,1), the function xxx has no maximum — it approaches 111 and never arrives.

Recap

  • Critical points are where f′=0f' = 0f′=0 or f′f'f′ fails to exist — candidates, not conclusions.
  • First derivative test: a sign change in f′f'f′ is the evidence. It always works.
  • Second derivative test is faster but says nothing when f′′(c)=0f''(c) = 0f′′(c)=0. Fall back to the first.
  • f′′>0f'' > 0f′′>0 is concave up, f′′<0f'' < 0f′′<0 concave down; an inflection needs a sign change, not merely a zero.
  • On a closed interval the extremes exist (EVT) and are found by comparing critical values and both endpoints.
